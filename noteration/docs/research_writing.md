## Executive Summary

The transition from consuming information to producing academic arguments requires a shift toward critical reading—the ability to evaluate how a text is argued rather than just what it says. Successful academic work is built upon purposeful research, which includes strategic previewing, skimming, and meticulous note-taking that prioritizes an author’s reasoning over isolated facts.

Central to all academic endeavors is academic integrity. Writers must distinguish their own ideas from those of experts through precise documentation systems (MLA, APA, Chicago, etc.) and by mastering the integration of quotations, paraphrases, and summaries. Furthermore, writing is not a monolithic skill; it varies significantly across disciplines. While science writing emphasizes concision and objective agency, history and literature prioritize close textual analysis and the navigation of primary versus secondary sources. Finally, all academic writing must be grounded in a clear, argumentative thesis that addresses a specific research question.

## I. Strategic Reading and Research Foundations

Academic writing depends entirely on the quality of the reading and research that precedes it. Efficiency in these stages prevents "patchwork" writing and ensures the author's own voice remains central.

### 1. Progressive Reading Techniques

Effective researchers do not read every text with the same intensity. They use a tiered approach to orient themselves.

* Previewing: Establishing a goal before reading. This involves examining the title, author background, publication date, and chapter headings to determine the text's relevance to the specific assignment or research question.
* Skimming: Gaining an overview of the logical progression. This is achieved by reading introductions, conclusions, and the first/last sentences of paragraphs while avoiding the distraction of unfamiliar terminology.
* Scanning: A tightly focused search for specific facts, figures, or mentions of a research topic.
* Critical Reading: The process of making judgments about how a text is argued. It requires "standing back" from the content to examine the thesis, context, reasoning, and evidence used by the author.

### 2. Principles of Note-Taking

Note-taking is the first step of drafting. The goal is to integrate sources into one's own thinking rather than merely recording information.

* Focus on the Research Question: Before taking detailed notes, researchers should analyze the assignment and formulate a specific research question (e.g., "how valid is the evidence" rather than just "what happened").
* Summarize Over Paraphrase: Word-for-word paraphrasing is often a waste of time. Instead, writers should compress ideas into their own words and only record exact quotes if the language is "memorably phrased or surprisingly expressed."
* Intelligent Labeling: Bibliographic information should be recorded immediately. Using separate sheets or cards for different topics allows for easier synthesis and "shuffling" of ideas later.

## II. The Mechanics of Academic Integrity

Plagiarism is defined as representing the ideas or expressions of another as one's own. Documentation is both a requirement for academic honesty and a courtesy that demonstrates a writer's expertise in their field.

### 1. When to Document

Documentation is required for:

* Direct quotations.
* Paraphrases and summaries.
* Specific ideas used as evidence.
* Distinctive or authoritative ideas, even if the writer disagrees with them.

Common Knowledge Exception: Facts easily found in standard reference books (e.g., the date of an armistice) or well-accepted interpretive ideas (e.g., smoking is harmful) generally do not require a specific source citation.

### 2. Summary of Documentation Systems

The choice of system depends on the discipline's priorities (e.g., author prominence vs. date of study).

| System | Primary Field | In-Text Style | Key Feature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MLA | Humanities | (Author Page) | Focus on "core elements" and containers (e.g., journals). |
| APA | Social Sciences | (Author, Date) | Emphasizes the timeliness of research; uses past tense for sources. |
| Chicago | History/Humanities | Superscript Numbers | Uses footnotes or endnotes; avoids parenthetical clutter. |
| Numbered | Sciences/Medical | [Number] | References listed in sequence of appearance. |

### 3. Integrating Quotations

Quotations should not "speak for themselves." They must be contextualized and followed by analysis.

* Short Quotations: Integrated into the text using a comma or colon, or blended using the conjunction "that."
* Block Quotations: Used for passages longer than four lines. These are indented, lack quotation marks, and are introduced by a full sentence and a colon.
* Alterations: Use an ellipsis (...) for omitted text and square brackets ([ ]) for any words added or changed for grammatical clarity.

## III. Disciplinary Conventions

Writing styles are dictated by the goals and methodologies of specific fields.

### 1. Writing in the Sciences

* Objective: Clear, concise communication of systematic enquiry.
* Voice: Traditionally passive ("The element was discovered"), though there is a modern shift toward active voice ("We found") to emphasize agency and transparency.
* Compression: Extensive use of technical jargon and abbreviations to save space for specialist audiences.
* Tense: Present tense for assertions about nature; past tense for describing specific procedures or previous studies.

### 2. Writing in History

* Primary vs. Secondary: Primary sources are artifacts from the time under study (newspapers, films, records); secondary sources are later interpretations by scholars.
* The Historical Question: Must be narrow enough to be manageable but broad enough to have significance. It should avoid "yes/no" answers, focusing instead on "how" and "why."
* Thesis: Must go beyond description to offer an argument that connects to previous historical work.

### 3. Writing in Literature

* Close Analysis: Focuses on language, imagery, and character development.
* Avoid Plot Summary: Assumes the reader knows the narrative; details are mentioned only as evidence for an argument.
* Speaker vs. Author: Recognizes that the narrator or "voice" in a poem is distinct from the author.

### 4. Public Writing

* Accessibility: Aims for non-specialist audiences. Jargon is avoided, and simpler, more direct language is preferred.
* Conventions: May use personal anecdotes, contractions, and first-person address. It focuses on present relevance and often omits formal introductions, conclusions, and literature reviews.

## IV. Specific Academic Genres

Academic assignments often require specific formats, each with its own purpose.

* The Abstract: A 100–250 word summary that answers: Why was the project done? What was done? What was found? What do the findings mean?
* The Book Review/Critique: Not a summary, but an evaluation. It situates a work within the theoretical concerns of a course and assesses its contribution to the field.
* The Annotated Bibliography: An alphabetical list of sources where each entry provides bibliographic data, a summary of the source's argument, and an assessment of its value to a specific research question.
* The Literature Review: A synthesis of what is known and unknown on a topic. It is organized by theme or trend, rather than as a list of summaries, to identify controversies and gaps in research.
* The Comparative Essay: Requires a clear "basis for comparison." It can be structured using the Alternating Method (point-by-point) for tight integration or the Block Method (subject-by-subject) when ideas about one subject build upon the other.
